The campaign

Is the CEO appraisal a considered judgement, or a collection of impressions?

The chief executive appraisal is the most consequential governance act a board performs each year. It is also, in most organisations, the least structured — a conversation shaped by the loudest recent event, the strength of individual relationships, and whoever speaks first.

This campaign offers Chairs and Managing Directors a structured, evidence‑based instrument for appraising chief‑executive performance: the CEOassess Framework in Governance Manager. It is deliberately not a consulting pitch. Boards are wary of outsiders inserting themselves into the CEO relationship, so the offer is an instrument the board runs itself — and only one dimension of it to begin with, which keeps the commitment to an hour. Facilitation is available only if wanted.

Value proposition

“Appraise the chief executive against defined dimensions, with evidence — not impressions gathered in a corridor.”

The offer

A free assessment against one dimension of the CEOassess Framework — the Chair's choice of the six.

The call to action

Assess one dimension in under an hour, then a 30‑minute confidential debrief with Pat Scally.

Why this lands with Chairs and Managing Directors

It names a known discomfort. Most Chairs privately concede the appraisal is less rigorous than it should be. Naming that without criticising them is the opening.

It reduces personal exposure. An appraisal that cannot be evidenced is hard to defend — in a dispute, at a remuneration decision, or if the CEO relationship ends badly.

It helps the CEO too. Framed as development rather than judgement, it gives the chief executive a structured view of strengths and growth areas — which makes it far easier for a Chair to introduce.

It is discreet. One dimension, under an hour, confidential to the Chair. Nothing enters the organisation's records unless the board chooses.

Positioning discipline: never imply the CEO is underperforming, and never imply the board is failing. The premise is that the people are capable and the instrument is missing — which is both true and the only framing a Chair will accept from an outsider.

Section one

Ideal customer profile

The best fit is an organisation with a genuine governing body, an annual appraisal obligation or expectation, and a Chair senior enough to introduce a new instrument without it reading as a challenge to the chief executive.

Attribute
Ideal profile
Sector
Local government, statutory authorities, government boards and commissions, member associations and peak bodies, not‑for‑profits, mutuals and co‑operatives, private and family companies with formal boards
Size
Revenue or budget $5m–$500m; 20–2,000 staff. Large enough for a real board, small enough that a global firm is not already retained
Structure
A functioning board or council with a Chair, President or Mayor; ideally a remuneration or people committee
Obligation context
An annual CEO review required by statute, constitution, funding agreement or the CEO's own contract
Trigger events
New Chair elected or appointed; CEO contract renewal or remuneration review approaching; a new CEO in their first year; an adverse governance review; board renewal or a recent director intake
Disqualifiers
Owner‑operator with no board; a CEO exit already in train (too late, and it reads as evidence‑building); an appraisal completed within the last two months

Buying roles

Primary — the champion

Chair, President, Mayor or Managing Director. Owns the appraisal, carries the exposure, and can authorise a free baseline without procurement or a board resolution.

Economic buyer

The Chair, or the remuneration / people committee chair. Signs for an ongoing annual assessment cycle once the first baseline proves useful.

Influencer — the secretariat

Company Secretary or Governance Manager. Usually the person who has to run the appraisal process, and therefore the strongest internal advocate for a structured instrument.

Blocker to expect

The CEO, if it reaches them as a surveillance instrument. Address early: position CEOassess as development and self‑awareness, and offer the CEO their own access.

Section two

The Chair, in their own terms

Chairs are experienced, time‑poor and protective of the CEO relationship. The message has to sound like it came from another Chair, not a vendor — and it must never put them in a position of appearing to distrust their chief executive.

What keeps them awake
An appraisal that couldn't be defended if it were challenged
Directors whose views are shaped by one recent event
Remuneration decisions with no documented basis
A CEO relationship that ends badly and publicly
Not knowing what the executive team actually experiences
Being the last to learn something the CEO already knew
What they want instead
A defined framework the whole board rates against
Evidence behind each judgement, on the record
A defensible basis for the remuneration recommendation
A conversation the CEO finds useful, not adversarial
Year‑on‑year comparability, not a fresh opinion annually
Less of their own time spent assembling the process

Objections and honest answers

Objection
Response
“We already do an annual review.”
Most boards do. The question is whether it rates defined dimensions with evidence, or records a discussion. CEOassess gives the same conversation a structure and a trend line.
“My CEO will think I don't trust them.”
That's why it's framed as development. Give the CEO their own access and their own maturity view — most welcome it, because they have never had a structured read on their own performance either.
“This is a board matter, not a software matter.”
Agreed. The judgement stays entirely with the board. The platform only supplies the framework, the scoring and the record — the parts that are currently done from memory.
“We'd want it facilitated independently.”
We do that too — Blue Zoo facilitates CEO reviews for WA agencies and local governments. But start with the free single-dimension assessment; facilitation is a separate decision.
“Confidentiality?”
The result is confidential to you as Chair. Nothing is shared with the organisation, the CEO or anyone else unless you choose to share it.

Section three

What CEOassess actually measures

Six connected dimensions of chief‑executive performance, each scored for maturity and for the confidence with which it could be evidenced — so a Chair can see both how strong a capability is and how well the judgement would stand up.

Corporate strategy
Vision, strategy and resource allocation — whether the CEO sets a direction the board recognises and funds it deliberately.
Organisational alignment
Talent, culture and organisational design — whether the organisation is shaped to deliver the strategy it has.
Team and processes
Teamwork, decision‑making and management processes — how the executive actually operates day to day.
Board engagement
Effectiveness, relationships and capability — the quality of what the board receives and how the CEO works with it.
External stakeholders
Social purpose, interactions and moments of truth — how the organisation is experienced by those outside it.
Personal work
Office environment, leadership model and personal perspective — the CEO's own practice, growth and self‑awareness.

The outcomes we can credibly promise

A defensible appraisal

Ratings against defined dimensions, with the evidence recorded — so the judgement can be explained to a director, a minister or a court.

Insight for the CEO

A structured view of leadership strengths and development areas, with recommendations the chief executive can actually act on.

Year‑on‑year comparability

The same dimensions rated the same way each cycle, so the board sees trajectory rather than an annual fresh opinion.

Reduced turnover risk

Clear expectations and honest, structured feedback are the most reliable protection against an unexpected and disruptive CEO exit.

Section four

Campaign mechanics

A four‑week sequence per cohort, run by the BDM with Pat Scally on every debrief. Chairs expect to deal with a principal, not a salesperson — so Pat fronts all conversations from the first call.

Week 1
Reach

Intro email from Pat to the cohort; LinkedIn posts 1–3; warm introductions through AICD and board networks.

Week 2
Engage

Follow‑up email; posts 4–6; connected calls to book dimension assessments. Minimum 20 connected calls.

Week 3
Assess

Dimension assessments run; posts 7–9; confidential profiles produced and debriefs scheduled.

Week 4
Convert

Confidential debriefs; posts 10–12; proposals for the annual cycle or a facilitated review.

One cohort per month. Time the cohort to the appraisal calendar — for local government and most agencies, that means approaching Chairs three to four months before the annual review falls due.
