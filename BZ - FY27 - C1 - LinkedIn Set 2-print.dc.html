Finding one
Decision rights that live on paper, not in practice

The single most common finding in public sector performance audits isn't fraud, and it isn't failure. It's ambiguity.

Org charts and terms of reference describe how decisions should be made. In practice, authority has drifted — approvals sit with whoever is available, and no one can say with confidence who owns a decision. That gap between governance on paper and governance in practice slows everything and quietly weakens accountability.

The fix is unglamorous: write down not just who is responsible, but who decides, who is consulted, and who is informed — and keep it current.

Finding two
Risk that reports, but doesn't decide

Almost every agency has a risk register. Far fewer can show that risk actually shapes what they fund, defer or escalate.

When risk becomes a quarterly reporting exercise, performance auditors find controls that exist on paper but don't change behaviour — and exposures that were known well in advance but never acted upon. The register is complete, yet the surprises keep coming.

Risk earns its place in governance only when it changes a decision.

Finding three
Activity reported as achievement

"We spent the budget and hit the milestones." In a performance audit, that is not the answer to the question being asked.

Findings routinely note programs reported by activity rather than outcome — a wall of green indicators sitting comfortably alongside services that are not actually improving for the people who rely on them. Performance audits ask the harder question: did it work?

If you cannot link expenditure to measurable public benefit, expect that to become a finding in itself.

Finding four
Procurement that can't demonstrate value

Procurement is one of the most frequently audited — and most frequently criticised — areas of public administration.

The common findings are rarely fraud: inadequate justification for direct purchases, incomplete documentation, undeclared conflicts of interest, evaluations that cannot be reconciled to the stated criteria. It is a control environment that cannot demonstrate a fair, transparent and value-driven process.

The remedy is a clean, contemporaneous record of why each significant purchase was made — one you can produce without scrambling.

Finding five
Findings accepted, but never closed

Perhaps the most telling finding of all is what happens after an audit.

Recommendations are accepted — and then overdue findings accumulate, year after year. Auditors read that register as a direct signal of how seriously an agency takes oversight, and the same issues tend to resurface until they do.

The remedy is simple discipline: give every finding an owner, a date and a status, and close it.
