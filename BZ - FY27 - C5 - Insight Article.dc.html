Evaluating the CEO: best practice performance appraisal for WA local government

Reviewing a council CEO is not a private-sector performance chat — it is a statutory process, a remuneration decision, and a matter of public record. Here is what a defensible review actually requires.

Blue Zoo Governance
9 min read
Local government advisory

Under the Local Government Act 1995 (WA), a council that employs a CEO for more than a year must review their performance at least once every year (ss 5.38–5.39). That obligation is easy to state and hard to discharge well. The CEO occupies a position unlike any other in the organisation — the sole employee of council, accountable to an elected body rather than a single line manager — and a review that is procedurally weak is a governance risk in its own right: open to challenge, hard to minute, and no basis for a defensible remuneration decision.

This article sets out what good practice looks like: the legislative floor a review must clear, the dimensions of CEO performance a council should actually assess, and the process model — independent, evidence-based, and built for an absolute majority resolution — that holds up to scrutiny.

The legislative floor
Local Government Act 1995, ss 5.38–5.39

Requires an annual review of CEO performance and sets the review as council's responsibility — not the Mayor or President's alone, and not the CEO's to self-manage.

Minimum standards for CEO performance review

The sector's minimum standards frame what a compliant review must include: agreed key result areas (KRAs), a documented review process, and a written assessment considered by council. They set a floor, not a ceiling — meeting them is not the same as running a good review.

Salaries and Allowances Tribunal — Local Government CEO determination

Sets the Total Reward Package bands CEO remuneration must sit within, banded by council classification. A performance review that will inform a remuneration decision needs to speak directly to where the CEO sits, and should sit, in the relevant SAT band.

What good practice actually assesses

Compliance sets the process; it does not tell council what a strong CEO performance actually looks like. A defensible appraisal draws on evidence across four areas.

01

Delivery against agreed KRAs

The review starts and ends with the key result areas council and the CEO agreed in writing at the start of the cycle — drawn from the council's strategic community plan and corporate business plan, not generic competencies. Without KRAs set and minuted up front, there is nothing objective to assess the CEO against, and the review collapses into opinion.

02

The council–CEO working relationship

Council sets strategic direction; the CEO runs the administration and advises council on that direction. A good review tests whether that line is respected — is council receiving frank, timely advice; is the CEO managing the administration without operational interference; are elected members able to speak candidly about the relationship in a confidential setting.

03

Organisational and community outcomes

Staff culture, service delivery, and community and stakeholder standing are all downstream of CEO leadership. An optional staff survey can surface organisational health the elected member survey cannot see, without turning the review into a popularity contest.

04

Remuneration in context

Where the review will inform remuneration, the assessment should be benchmarked against the applicable Salaries and Allowances Tribunal band for the council's classification — not negotiated in isolation from performance evidence.

A review that cannot be minuted, explained, and defended is not a compliant one — regardless of how thorough the conversation behind it was.

The two failure modes

Most reviews that come unstuck fail in one of two directions.

Too informal

A closed-door conversation between the CEO and the Mayor or President, undocumented, with no survey evidence and no council resolution. Efficient, but indefensible if challenged — and often unfair to the CEO, who has no structured input into their own assessment.

Compromised independence

Run in-house by a CEO's own staff, or by a councillor with a personal stake in the outcome. Even a rigorous process loses credibility if the person running it cannot be seen to be impartial — and every finding becomes contestable.

A model that holds up: how Blue Zoo runs the review

Council keeps ownership of the KRAs and the final endorsement. An independent reviewer runs everything in between — on the record, at arm's length from both the CEO and any one councillor.

1
Agree the KRAs
Council and CEO agree key result areas in writing for the cycle, anchored to the strategic community plan.
2
Elected member survey
Every councillor gives confidential, structured input against the KRAs and the working relationship — not just the Mayor or President's view.
3
Staff survey (optional)
Adds an organisational-health signal from within the administration, where council wants that evidence.
4
SAT remuneration benchmarking
Where relevant, positions the CEO's Total Reward Package against the applicable Salaries and Allowances Tribunal band.
5
Confidential report to council
A written assessment is presented to council and endorsed by absolute majority resolution — minuted, evidence-based, and ready to withstand scrutiny.

Done this way, the review does what it is meant to do: give the CEO a fair, evidence-based assessment; give council a defensible record; and settle remuneration against an objective band rather than a negotiation.
