The campaign

How many of your policies are current, known and actually followed?

Almost every organisation has a policy library. Very few can say, with evidence, how much of it is current, who owns each document, whether staff understand the policies that apply to them, or whether practice matches what the policy says. The library exists; the governance around it usually does not.

This campaign offers governance and corporate services leadership an objective read on policy governance using the POLICYassess Framework in Governance Manager. It is deliberately not a document‑management pitch. These leaders already have intranets and content systems; what they lack is a defensible measure of whether the policy framework is doing its job — which is what an auditor, a regulator or a post‑incident review examines first.

Value proposition

“Refine policy governance so policy shapes what people actually do — not just what the library contains.”

The offer

A free assessment against one domain of the POLICYassess Framework — their choice of the four.

The call to action

Assess one domain in under an hour, then a 30‑minute debrief with a Blue Zoo adviser.

Why this lands with governance and corporate services leaders

It is their actual problem. Policy currency, ownership and coverage sit squarely in this role, and most holders privately know the library has drifted. Naming it without blame is the opening.

It arrives before the audit. Internal and performance audits routinely test policy currency, ownership and awareness. Seeing what a reviewer will see is straightforwardly useful.

One domain is an easy yes. A whole‑framework review reads as a project they cannot resource. One domain, chosen by them, reads as an hour.

It produces something usable. A maturity profile and ranked gap list strengthens the resourcing case they are usually asked to make with no evidence behind it.

Positioning discipline: never imply the policy library is a mess — this person almost certainly inherited it and has been asking for resources to fix it. The premise is that the documents are sound and the governance around them is unmeasured, which makes us an ally in an argument they are already having internally.

Section one

Ideal customer profile

The best fit is an organisation with a policy library large enough to have drifted, real regulatory scrutiny, and a named governance or corporate services owner who is accountable for policy but rarely resourced for it.

Attribute
Ideal profile
Sector
Local government, state agencies, statutory authorities, health and education, utilities, member associations and peak bodies, not‑for‑profits, regulated private companies
Size
100–5,000 staff; a policy library of roughly 40–500 documents. Large enough to have drifted, small enough that there is no dedicated policy team
Structure
A Governance Manager, Company Secretary or Corporate Services function that owns the policy framework; an audit and risk committee
Regulatory context
Policy obligations flowing from statute, funding agreements, accreditation standards, WHS duties, privacy and records legislation, or a sector code of practice
Trigger events
An audit finding on policy currency; a machinery‑of‑government change or merger; new legislation requiring policy response; accreditation approaching; a new governance or corporate services leader; an incident where the policy was found not to have been followed
Disqualifiers
Fewer than ~20 policies; no named policy owner; a policy review already underway with an external firm engaged

Buying roles

Primary — the champion

Governance Director or Manager, Company Secretary, Manager Governance & Risk. Owns the framework, feels the drift daily, and can authorise a free single‑domain assessment without procurement.

Economic buyer

Director or Manager Corporate Services, Chief Operating Officer, Executive Director Corporate. Signs for an ongoing assessment pack once the first domain proves useful.

Influencer — assurance

Chief Audit Executive, Risk Manager, audit and risk committee chair. Reliably supportive, because evidenced policy governance closes a finding they raise every cycle.

Blocker to expect

An IT or records manager who hears “policy” and assumes we are pitching a document management system. Correct early: we assess governance, not storage.

Section three

What POLICYassess actually measures

Four governance domains, each scored for maturity and for the confidence with which it could be evidenced — so a governance leader can see both how strong a practice is and how well it would stand up to review. The free offer covers one domain of their choosing.

Policy development & clarity
Policies are developed with clear objectives and defined scope, so each one is understandable, actionable and unambiguous to the people who must apply it.
Compliance & alignment
Policies comply with relevant laws and regulations and align with the organisation's strategic objectives — so legislative change has a route into the framework.
Engagement & implementation
Stakeholders are engaged in development and implementation, so policies are understood and adopted rather than published and ignored.
Monitoring & improvement
Policy effectiveness is monitored and policies are revised in response to feedback, performance and changing circumstances — a living framework, not a static library.

The outcomes we can credibly promise

Enhanced strategic alignment

Policies that support and advance the organisation's strategic objectives, rather than sitting alongside them.

Improved compliance & risk management

Policies that meet legal requirements and manage risk effectively, reducing exposure to penalties and adverse findings.

Increased engagement & understanding

Greater stakeholder involvement and clearer communication, so policies are genuinely understood and followed.

Dynamic policy management

Continuous monitoring and revision keeps policies relevant and effective as circumstances change.

Campaign mechanics

A four‑week sequence per cohort, run by the BDM with Pat Scally on the debriefs. Everything is tracked in HubSpot against the standard deal stages.

Week 1
Reach

Intro email to the cohort; LinkedIn posts 1–3; connection requests to governance and corporate services leaders.

Week 2
Engage

Follow‑up email; posts 4–6; connected calls to book domain assessments. Minimum 20 connected calls.

Week 3
Assess

Domain assessments run; posts 7–9; profiles produced and debriefs scheduled.

Week 4
Convert

30‑minute debriefs; posts 10–12; proposals for the remaining domains or an ongoing assessment pack.

One cohort per month. Time cohorts to the audit calendar — approach two to three months before internal audit's policy review lands, while there is still time to act on findings. Governance Institute and local government governance networks are the fastest routes to a warm list.
