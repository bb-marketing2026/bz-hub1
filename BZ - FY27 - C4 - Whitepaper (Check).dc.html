Beyond compliance: the indicators that governance has improved capability

Twelve observable signs that stronger governance has produced a more capable, more resilient agency — not merely a more compliant one.

Introduction

When has governance actually made a difference?

Every Director General has been asked some version of the question, and few can answer it cleanly: has all the effort we have poured into governance actually made the agency better? We can readily show that we are compliant. Demonstrating that we are more capable is far harder — and far more important.

This paper is written for that audience. It does not restate why compliance matters; that case is well made and widely accepted. It addresses the question that comes next: once an agency has invested in governance, how would you know whether it has genuinely lifted capability and resilience, rather than simply added another layer of process?

The distinction is not academic. Capability is what allows an agency to make better decisions, absorb shocks and keep improving. Compliance protects against failure; capability produces performance. Governance is worth the investment only to the extent that it builds the second, and the evidence for that is often overlooked because it is subtle.

What follows is a practical set of twelve indicators — the observable signs that governance has moved an agency beyond compliance. They are grouped by where they show up: in decisions, in the handling of risk and assurance, and in outcomes, culture and resilience.

Compliance can be certified. Capability can only be observed — in how an agency decides, how it handles risk, and how it recovers when something goes wrong.

The measurement problem

Why capability is so easy to miss

The reason capability is under-measured is straightforward: compliance is designed to be counted, and capability is not. A control is either in place or it is not; a deadline is either met or missed. Capability leaves no such tidy record. It shows up in the quality of a decision, the timeliness of a warning, the speed of a recovery — none of which fit neatly into a register.

So agencies measure what is measurable and quietly assume the rest. A dashboard full of green indicators feels like evidence of health, but it reports only that the machinery ran. Whether the machinery produced better outcomes is a different question, and one that most reporting is not built to answer.

The consequence is a blind spot at the top of the organisation. Executives receive abundant assurance that obligations were met and very little on whether the agency is becoming more capable. When resources are tight, this matters: governance effort that does not lift capability is cost without return, and no one can see it clearly enough to redirect it.

The remedy is not another framework. It is a shift in what leaders look for — from confirmation that rules were followed to evidence that judgement, foresight and resilience have improved. That evidence exists; it is simply qualitative, distributed, and easy to walk past.

The indicators in this paper are chosen because they are observable without a new reporting system. A Director General can look for each of them in the material that already crosses the desk — board papers, risk reports, audit responses, and the way people talk about their work.

?

A useful reframe: stop asking "are we compliant?" and start asking "what can this agency do now that it could not do three years ago?" The answer, or its absence, is your capability measure.

The twelve indicators · Decisions & strategy

Governance shows up in how decisions are made

01Decisions cite evidence, not just authority

In a compliant agency, a decision is justified by who made it and which step was followed. In a capable one, the record shows what evidence was weighed and why the chosen option was preferred. When board papers routinely set out options, trade-offs and the basis for the recommendation, governance has begun to shape thinking rather than merely document it.

02Strategy changes when the evidence changes

A capable agency treats its strategy as a live instrument, adjusted as risk, performance and circumstance shift. If plans are set annually and then defended regardless of what the data later shows, governance is decorative. The willingness to revise course — visibly, and on the record — is one of the clearest signs that information is genuinely informing direction.

03Trade-offs are made openly, not avoided

Weak governance lets hard choices drift, funding everything a little and nothing well. Stronger governance forces the trade-off into the open: this, therefore not that. When executives can point to decisions where they consciously stopped or deprioritised work to protect what mattered most, the agency is exercising judgement, not just processing demand.

04Governance information arrives in time to matter

Capability is partly a question of timing. Risk, performance and assurance information is only valuable if it reaches decision-makers before the decision is taken. If reports consistently confirm what has already happened, governance is a rear-view mirror. When it shapes choices while they are still open, it has become a steering instrument.

?

Look here first: read your last three board papers as an outsider. Do they show reasoning and choice — or only compliance with a template? The difference is visible in minutes.

The twelve indicators · Risk & assurance

Capability shows up in how risk is handled

05Risk is discussed before events, not after

In a compliant agency, risk registers are updated to satisfy a cycle and consulted after something goes wrong. In a capable one, risk is a standing part of the conversation about what to do next. When leadership can describe risks it acted on before they materialised — funding deferred, a project paused, a control strengthened — foresight has replaced paperwork.

06Audit findings fall in severity and recur less

The single most objective indicator is the trajectory of findings. A capable agency closes recommendations, and the findings that remain are less severe and less repetitive year on year. Where the same significant issues reappear cycle after cycle, governance has not translated into action, whatever the frameworks say.

07The agency surfaces its own problems

Perhaps the truest sign of capability is that bad news travels upward quickly. In a compliance culture, problems are managed until an auditor finds them; in a capable one, staff raise issues early because doing so is rewarded, not punished. An agency that discovers most of its own problems before anyone else does is governing well.

08Assurance effort is matched to risk

Mature governance directs scrutiny where it is most needed rather than spreading it evenly for appearance. When internal audit plans visibly follow the risk profile — heavier where exposure is greatest, lighter where it is not — assurance has become a management tool. Uniform, box-ticking coverage is a sign the agency is still assuring for compliance, not capability.

"You can measure compliance by what an agency reports. You measure capability by what it catches before anyone has to report it."

The twelve indicators · Outcomes, culture & resilience

Capability shows up in outcomes and resilience

09Reporting shifts from activity to outcomes

Watch what leadership reports with pride. A compliant agency reports activity — projects delivered, training completed, milestones met. A capable one reports change — the waiting time that fell, the error rate that dropped, the service that reached more people. When the outcome, not the effort, is the headline, capability has taken hold.

10Staff can connect their work to purpose

Ask people two levels below the executive what their work is for. In a compliant culture the answer is procedural; in a capable one it reaches the agency's purpose and the public it serves. That line of sight, held widely, is a governance achievement — it means direction has travelled from the board to the frontline intact.

11Improvement continues without a program driving it

Capability is proven by what happens after the initiative ends. If governance improvements regress the moment a project closes, they were never embedded. When teams keep refining how they work as an ordinary habit — reviewing, adjusting, learning — improvement has become part of the operating rhythm rather than a campaign.

12The agency recovers faster from disruption

Resilience is capability under stress. A capable agency absorbs a shock — a system failure, a funding cut, a critical report — and returns to function quickly, learning as it goes. Recovery time is among the most honest measures of governance there is, because it cannot be faked and it cannot be borrowed the week before it is needed.

Making it measurable

Turning the indicators into evidence

Indicators become useful when they are tracked deliberately. Capability has both leading signs — early evidence that judgement and foresight are improving — and lagging signs, which confirm the improvement after the fact. A balanced view watches both.

Leading signs — watch monthly
  • Board papers that show options and reasoning
  • Risks acted on before they materialise
  • Problems raised by staff, not found by auditors
  • Decisions to stop or deprioritise work
  • Assurance effort concentrated on real exposure
Lagging signs — review yearly
  • Fewer, less severe and less repeated findings
  • Measurable improvement in service outcomes
  • Shorter recovery time after disruption
  • Rising governance maturity scores
  • Improvement sustained without a program

A maturity model ties the two together. By rating each governance domain against defined levels — and scoring both how well a practice is embedded and how readily it can be evidenced — an agency converts these qualitative signals into a repeatable measure it can track over time and benchmark against peers.

The discipline matters more than the instrument. An agency that reviews a handful of honest leading indicators every month, and confirms them against lagging measures once a year, will see its capability trajectory clearly — and will know whether its governance investment is earning its keep.

Reading the signals

What this means for a Director General

A Director General does not need a new report to apply any of this. The evidence is already arriving — in board papers, risk updates, audit responses and the language people use about their work. What changes is the question held in mind while reading them: not "did we comply?" but "is this the work of a more capable agency?"

Used this way, the indicators become a quiet diagnostic. A run of board papers that show genuine reasoning, a downward trend in repeat findings, staff who surface problems early, a service outcome that has measurably improved — each is a data point, and together they form a picture no compliance report can provide.

The absence of these signs is equally informative. An agency that is flawlessly compliant yet shows none of them has a governance system that protects but does not improve. That is not a failure of compliance; it is a ceiling on capability — and it is precisely the pattern that a maturity assessment is designed to expose and address.

For leaders under real resource pressure, this is the practical value. Knowing which governance effort is lifting capability, and which is merely adding process, lets scarce attention be spent where it changes outcomes — and lets a Director General answer, with evidence, the question they are always eventually asked.

"The most capable agencies are rarely the most decorated for compliance. They are the ones that would recover quickest if everything went wrong tomorrow."

In closing

From compliant to genuinely capable

Compliance will always be necessary, and no agency should apologise for taking it seriously. But it is the floor, not the measure of ambition. The agencies that serve the public best are those whose governance has made them more capable — better at deciding, at anticipating, and at recovering.

The twelve indicators in this paper are offered as a way to see that capability where it has previously been invisible. None requires new machinery. Each can be read in the evidence an executive already receives, and together they answer the question that matters: has our governance made us better, or merely safer?

The move from compliance to capability is, in the end, a discipline of attention — looking for the right signals, measuring them honestly, and acting on what they reveal. It is the difference between an agency that satisfies its obligations and one that earns the enduring confidence of the public it serves.
