You already use AI. Do you govern it?

Almost every public sector agency is now using AI — drafting, summarising, analysing. Far fewer can say who approved it, where the data goes, or who is accountable when it gets something wrong.

That gap between use and oversight is where risk quietly accumulates, and it is exactly where the next adverse audit finding is most likely to sit. The Auditor General has already signalled that AI governance is in scope.

The board question is not whether your agency uses AI. It is whether your agency can explain, defend and account for how it does.

Who is accountable when the AI is wrong?

AI does not dilute accountability. The agency remains responsible for every decision it informs — yet ownership is often split between the business area, the technology team and the vendor, which usually means no one owns it at all.

"The system decided" is not a defence a public agency can offer. Naming a single accountable owner for each significant AI system is what turns a diffuse anxiety about AI into a manageable responsibility.

Could you name, today, who is answerable for every AI system in use across your agency?

Post three
Can you explain an AI-assisted decision?

Public decisions must be capable of explanation. If an agency cannot describe, in plain terms, how an AI system reached a result, it cannot defend that result to a citizen, a minister or a court.

Opacity is a governance risk, not a technical detail. If a decision cannot be explained, it cannot truly be reviewed — and unreviewable decisions are exactly what performance audits are built to find.

For every AI use that touches a person, ask: could we explain it, and did we record that a human made the call?

Post four
Every prompt is data leaving the desk

When staff paste information into a public AI tool, that data leaves the agency — and may be retained, or used to train an external model. Where the tool is provided by a third party, the agency's obligations do not transfer with it.

Data sovereignty, privacy and security should be settled before adoption, not after an incident. A recognised standard such as ISO/IEC 42001 offers a ready framework; the real question is whether your agency is using one at all.

Do you know where the information entered into AI tools across your agency actually goes?
