Preparing for non-financial performance audits

How WA agencies can be ready for the audits that examine not the numbers, but whether the public received value.

By Pat Scally · Chief Executive Officer, Blue Zoo

For most Western Australian agencies, the annual financial audit is a familiar routine. The performance audit — sometimes called a non-financial audit — is a different, and less comfortable, exercise. It does not ask whether the accounts are accurate. It asks whether a program was well governed, whether decisions were sound, and whether the public received value for the money spent.

What a performance audit actually examines

Where a financial audit tests figures and controls, a performance audit tests judgement. The Auditor General examines how an agency set its objectives, how it made and documented decisions, how it understood and managed risk, and — above all — whether the intended outcomes were achieved. It is an examination of governance in action, not of the ledger.

That shift changes what "being ready" means. The evidence a performance audit looks for is rarely a reconciled balance; it is a clear trail showing who decided what, on what basis, and to what effect. Agencies that can produce that trail without reconstructing it are already most of the way prepared.

Why capable agencies still get caught out

The most common reason agencies are unprepared is that they have prepared for the wrong audit. Confident after a clean financial opinion, they assume that sound administration will speak for itself. It rarely does. A clean set of accounts and a well-run program are different achievements, and only the second is what a performance audit is looking for.

Performance auditors ask questions that routine compliance never surfaces. Can you show the program actually worked? Can you explain why this option was chosen over the alternatives? Can you demonstrate that the risks were understood before the event, not rationalised after it? Agencies that have never rehearsed these questions tend to answer them for the first time in front of the auditor — the worst possible moment to start.

How to prepare

Six things to do before the audit is announced

01Know what is likely to be examined

Understand the Auditor General's current focus areas and how your agency's major programs map to them. Performance audit topics are rarely a surprise to those who are paying attention. Knowing where the scrutiny is likely to fall lets you look there first.

02Put the evidence trail in order

Decisions, approvals, advice and records should together tell a coherent story without needing to be reconstructed. If the reasoning behind a significant decision lives only in someone's memory or a lost email thread, it does not yet exist for audit purposes.

03Show outcomes, not just activity

Be able to link what was spent to the benefit the public received. Auditors are unimpressed by lists of tasks completed; they want evidence that the program achieved what it set out to.

04Close outstanding recommendations

Nothing signals weak governance faster than accepted findings left unactioned year after year. Before an audit begins, work through your register of open recommendations and close what you can. It is among the first things an auditor will check.

05Clarify decision rights

Be able to name who was accountable for each significant decision, and show that they had the authority to make it. Ambiguity about who decided what is among the most common and damaging findings an audit reports.

06Rehearse the narrative

Leaders should be able to explain, plainly and consistently, why the program exists, what it was meant to achieve, and what it delivered.

A simple test: pick your largest program and ask whether, this afternoon, you could assemble the evidence to show it was well governed and delivered value. If the answer is "not without a scramble", the time to prepare is now — not when the audit is announced.

Preparing for a performance audit is not about surviving scrutiny. It is about running programs well enough that scrutiny holds no fear. The agencies that fare best are simply those that ask themselves the auditor's questions first, on their own timetable.
