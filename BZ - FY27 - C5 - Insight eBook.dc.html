Abstract

Why the CEO review is different in the public sector

In a Western Australian local government, the Chief Executive Officer is the single point of accountability between an elected Council and the administration that serves it. Reviewing that role is not a private-sector performance conversation. It is a statutory obligation, a remuneration decision, and a matter of public record — and a review that is procedurally weak is a governance risk in its own right.

This article reframes best-practice CEO appraisal for the local government context. It sets out the statutory framework a review must satisfy — the Local Government Act 1995, the sector's minimum standards, and the Salaries and Allowances Tribunal's determination on Elected Member and CEO remuneration — and then walks through a defensible methodology, from setting the performance criteria to endorsing the outcome by resolution of Council.

Throughout, the emphasis is the same one that separates a credible review from a contestable one: independence, evidence, and a record that can be minuted, explained and defended.

The statutory framework

A review with legal weight

Unlike the private sector, where appraisal practice is a matter of governance preference, a local government CEO review is anchored in legislation. Under the Local Government Act 1995 (WA), a council that employs a CEO for more than a year must review that person's performance at least once in every year of their contract (ss 5.38–5.39). The obligation rests with the Council as a whole — not the Mayor or President acting alone, and not the CEO managing their own assessment.

Three instruments frame what a compliant, defensible review must do. They set a floor; meeting them is the beginning of good practice, not the end of it.

Local Government Act 1995 — ss 5.38–5.39

Requires an annual review of the CEO's performance and places responsibility with the Council. Any decision that flows from the review — including endorsement and remuneration — is a decision of Council, taken at a properly convened meeting.

The sector's minimum standards for CEO performance review

The prescribed standards require agreed performance criteria, a documented review process undertaken with the CEO, and a written outcome considered by Council. They exist to ensure fairness and consistency across all 139 WA local governments — from the smallest shire to the largest city.

SAT Elected Member and CEO Remuneration Determination

The Salaries and Allowances Tribunal determines the Total Reward Package band within which a CEO's remuneration must sit, according to the council's classification. A review that will inform remuneration must speak directly to where the CEO sits, and should sit, within the applicable band.

The consequence of this framework is simple but often underestimated: the review is not a conversation that happens to be written up afterwards. It is a formal process whose every step — the criteria, the evidence, the analysis and the resolution — must be capable of standing up to scrutiny from Elected Members, the CEO, the Department and, ultimately, the community.

Setting the performance criteria

You can only assess what was agreed

A defensible review begins long before the survey period — at the point where Council and the CEO agree, in writing, the key performance indicators for the year. In a local government these are not generic leadership competencies. They are drawn from the instruments Council is itself accountable for: the Strategic Community Plan, the Corporate Business Plan, the annual budget, and any specific priorities Council has resolved for the term.

Where KPIs are set and minuted at the start of the cycle, the review has something objective to measure against. Where they are not, the review collapses into opinion — and opinion, however sincerely held, is neither fair to the CEO nor defensible for Council.

What good performance criteria cover
Strategic delivery
Progress against the Strategic Community Plan and the priorities Council has set for the year.
Financial stewardship
Sound management of the budget, long-term financial plan and the community's assets.
Organisational leadership
Culture, capability and the health of the administration the CEO leads.
Governance & relationships
Frank, timely advice to Council and a working relationship that respects the line between the two roles.

The criteria should be paired with behavioural expectations — how the CEO leads, not only what they deliver. Behavioural questions surface the things a KPI cannot: whether Elected Members receive advice without fear or favour, whether staff experience consistent and respectful leadership, and whether the CEO manages the administration without drawing Council into operational matters.

Agreeing these criteria openly, at the start of the cycle, does more than enable the review. It tells the CEO precisely what Council values, and gives the whole of Council a shared reference point when the assessment is eventually tabled.

The appraisal methodology

A defined sequence, start to finish

Best practice runs the review as a defined, repeatable sequence — from the opening meeting with the Mayor, Deputy Mayor and CEO through to the report being referred to Governance for the next agenda. The methodology below is the model Blue Zoo follows: seventeen steps that keep the process on the record and on the calendar.

Before the cycle begins, Blue Zoo can also review the council's CEO Performance Review Policy and/or the CEO Performance Review Committee Terms of Reference — checking them for compliance with the Act and the sector's minimum standards, and confirming they remain fit for purpose before the review gets under way.

Two entry points. The engagement can begin with a CEO review and then set the KPIs for the following year, or Blue Zoo can commence at the start of the cycle with KPI development. Either way, the process is tailored to accommodate Council policy and preference.

Gathering the evidence

Three sources, one balanced picture

A credible assessment triangulates evidence rather than resting on a single voice. In the local government model, three structured sources combine to give Council a balanced, defensible picture of the CEO's performance across the cycle.

A
The Elected Member Survey

Every councillor — not only the Mayor or President — gives confidential, structured input against the agreed KPIs and against behavioural expectations. The survey presents the KPI results together with the CEO's own commentary, so Elected Members respond to evidence rather than impression. This is the single most important safeguard of fairness in the process.

B
The Staff Survey

Focused on behavioural questions and organisational health, the staff survey surfaces the culture and leadership signals that Elected Members cannot see from the Council table. It is presented to the Mayor and Deputy Mayor for approval before release, and is weighted as one input among several — never allowed to become a popularity contest.

C
Remuneration data

Requested from Finance early and benchmarked against the Salaries and Allowances Tribunal band for the council's classification. This grounds any remuneration discussion in the CEO's position within the determined range, rather than in negotiation detached from performance.

Independence is what makes the evidence usable. When surveys are designed, distributed and analysed at arm's length — not by the CEO's own staff, and not by a councillor with a personal stake — every finding becomes harder to contest and easier for Council to stand behind.

From findings to a defensible outcome

A report Council can stand behind

Once the survey period closes, the evidence is analysed and drafted into a report. Best practice keeps this stage deliberate and transparent. A draft is presented to the Mayor, Deputy Mayor and CEO with an invitation to comment; those comments are received and integrated into a final draft; and only then is the final report presented for consideration. The CEO is never surprised by the outcome, and the record shows they were given a genuine opportunity to respond.

Analyse & draft
Survey results, KPI outcomes and remuneration benchmarking are synthesised into a clear, evidence-based draft report.
Invite comment
The draft is shared with the Mayor, Deputy Mayor and CEO; feedback is sought and integrated before the report is finalised.
Endorse
The final report is considered by Council and endorsed by an absolute majority resolution — the decision of the elected body, properly minuted.
Refer & record
The outcome is referred to Governance for inclusion in the next agenda, closing the loop and setting up the next cycle.

The remuneration review runs alongside this drafting, so that any adjustment to the CEO's Total Reward Package is made with the performance evidence in front of Council and within the applicable Salaries and Allowances Tribunal band. Performance and remuneration are considered together, but the remuneration decision follows the evidence — not the other way around.

A review that cannot be minuted, explained and defended is not a compliant one — regardless of how thorough the conversation behind it was.

Summary

Getting the CEO review right

In local government, the CEO performance review is where accountability, fairness and public trust meet. Done well, it gives the CEO a clear, evidence-based assessment; gives Council a defensible record; and settles remuneration against an objective band rather than a negotiation. Done poorly — informally, or without independence — it becomes a governance risk that can be challenged, and a source of tension between Council and its most senior employee.

The difference is method. Agree the criteria openly. Gather evidence from Elected Members, staff and Finance at arm's length. Analyse it fairly, invite comment, and endorse the outcome by resolution of Council. Keep every step on the record and on the calendar. That is the practice this article has set out, and the model Blue Zoo brings to every review it runs.
