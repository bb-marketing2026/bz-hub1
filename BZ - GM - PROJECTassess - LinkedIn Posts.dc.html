A steering committee that received status instead of choices. Resourcing agreed verbally across competing priorities. Benefits promised but never defined measurably. Post 02 · Decision rights

A test worth running on your own project this week. Ask three people who can approve a change to scope. Then ask them who approves it. If you get three different answers, you don't have a decision-rights problem yet — you have one waiting for the first contested decision. Clarity of decision rights is the first thing PROJECTassess measures, because it is the first thing that fails. Free assessment on one domain on one project. Post 03 · Steering committees

Most steering committees don't steer. They receive a status pack, note the RAG ratings, thank the project team and adjourn. Nothing is decided, so nothing is owned — and the decisions that mattered get made in corridors between meetings. A steering committee that makes decisions on the record is worth more to a Project Director than any reporting template. If yours doesn't, that's a governance gap, and it's a fixable one. Post 04 · The gateway review

Gateway reviews don't fail projects. They discover things. The uncomfortable part is that the reviewer sees your project governance for the first time on the same day you do — because nobody measured it beforehand. Seeing what the reviewer will see, while you still have time to act, is a materially better position. Post 05 · Strategic alignment

"Aligned to strategy" is the most-asserted and least-evidenced claim in project governance. Every business case says it. Very few can show how the project was selected and prioritised against strategic value, or what would have had to be true for it to be deprioritised. If your portfolio can't demonstrate the selection logic, the portfolio isn't prioritised — it's accumulated. Strategic alignment is one of the five capabilities PROJECTassess measures. Post 06 · Resource conflicts

The most expensive resource conflict on your portfolio is the one nobody wrote down. Two projects were promised the same specialist. Both plans assumed they had them. The conflict surfaces six weeks later as a slipped milestone with no obvious cause. Resource governance isn't a scheduling tool problem. It's a decision-rights and tracking problem. PROJECTassess scores how well resources are planned, tracked and optimised across projects — including whether commitments are recorded at all. Post 07 · Above the method

A fair question I get from Project Directors: "We already use PRINCE2. Why would we assess governance separately?" Because delivery method and governance quality are different things. A method tells you how to run the project. Governance determines whether the right project was chosen, who decides what, whether resources are genuinely committed, and whether anyone would know early if it went wrong. PROJECTassess sits above the method — it works whether you run PRINCE2, PMBOK, agile or your own framework. Free assessment on one domain on one project.

Post 08 · Compliance timing

There are two ways to handle project compliance. One: track obligations through the lifecycle, so the evidence exists when it's asked for. Two: assemble it in the fortnight before the review, from memory and email archaeology. Most projects do the second, then describe it as the first. Compliance monitoring is the fifth capability PROJECTassess measures — not because compliance is the goal, but because scrambling for evidence is a symptom of governance that isn't operating. Post 09 · Capability vs evidence

Here's a test that separates confident project governance from defensible project governance. Take any governance practice you're sure about. Now ask: if an auditor asked us to prove this today, what would we hand over? A strong practice with weak evidence isn't good governance yet. It's good intentions waiting to be documented. PROJECTassess scores every element twice — once for how well the practice is embedded, once for how readily you could prove it. The gap between them is usually the most useful finding. Post 10 · Time to value

Better project governance isn't about adding control. It's about shortening time to value. Clear decision rights mean decisions happen in days, not fortnights. Tracked resource commitments mean fewer silent slippages. A steering committee that actually decides removes the queue of things waiting on someone. Every one of those is time recovered — which is why governance maturity and delivery speed move together, not against each other. Post 11 · The portfolio view

One project's governance score is interesting. A portfolio's is actionable. Assess several projects against the same framework and patterns appear immediately: the capability that's weak everywhere (a structural gap, usually a missing standard), and the one that varies wildly (capability exists somewhere in your organisation — connect the strong to the weak). The second is the cheapest improvement available to a PMO, and it's invisible without measurement. Start with one domain on one project, free. Scale to the portfolio when it proves useful.
