Post one
Know what is likely to be examined

Most performance audit topics are not a surprise. The Auditor General signals focus areas well in advance — the agencies that get caught out are simply the ones who weren't looking.

Before an audit is announced, ask a simple question: which of our major programs would sit squarely in the current areas of scrutiny? Then look there first, honestly, while you still have time to act.

Preparation isn't about predicting the exact audit. It's about knowing where the light is most likely to fall.

Post two
Put the evidence trail in order

Here's a test worth running on your largest program: could you show, this afternoon, who decided what, on what basis, and to what effect — without reconstructing it?

If the reasoning behind a significant decision lives only in someone's memory or a lost email thread, it doesn't yet exist for audit purposes. A performance audit doesn't want a reconciled balance. It wants a clear, coherent trail.

The agencies that fare best aren't the ones who scramble to assemble that trail. They're the ones who never had to.

Post three
Show outcomes, not just activity

Auditors are unimpressed by lists of tasks completed. They want evidence that the program achieved what it set out to.

It's one of the most uncomfortable moments in a performance audit: being asked not what you did, but what changed as a result — and having only activity to point to. A wall of green indicators is not the same as a better outcome for the public.

If you can't measure the outcome, expect that to become a finding in itself.

Post four
Close outstanding recommendations

Nothing signals weak governance faster than accepted audit findings left unactioned, year after year.

It's often the first thing an auditor checks — and a growing register of open recommendations tells them exactly how seriously the organisation takes oversight. The fix isn't glamorous, but it's reliable: assign each finding an owner, a date and a status, and close it.

Do it before the next audit begins, not during it.

Post five
Clarify decision rights

"Who was accountable for this decision?" It sounds simple. In a performance audit, it's often where things unravel.

When authority is ambiguous — when no one can say with confidence who decided what, and whether they had the standing to — it undermines confidence in everything else the agency did. Clear decision rights are among the cheapest safeguards you can put in place, and their absence is among the most common findings.

Be able to name the decision-maker, and show they had the authority. Before you're asked.
