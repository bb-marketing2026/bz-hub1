Using governance maturity models

How WA agencies can measure governance with evidence rather than opinion — and turn the result into a roadmap Boards can act on.

By Pat Scally · Chief Executive Officer, Blue Zoo

Agencies receive a financial dashboard every month. They rarely receive an equivalent measure of how well the organisation is governed. Governance quality stays a matter of opinion — confident in some rooms, anxious in others, and almost never comparable from one year to the next. A governance maturity model closes that gap. It replaces impressions with a structured, repeatable measure of how governance is actually working.

Level 1
Initial

Informal and person-dependent; little documentation.

Level 2
Developing

Basic processes exist but are applied inconsistently.

Level 3
Established

Documented, consistent and backed by reliable evidence.

Level 4
Managed

Monitored with measures; improvement is continuous.

Level 5
Leading

Embedded in culture; benchmarked to leading practice.

What a maturity model actually measures

A maturity model describes what good governance looks like at successive stages, from informal and reactive through to embedded and self-improving. Each governance domain — strategic direction, oversight, risk, assurance, accountability — is rated against those stages, so leaders can see not just whether a practice exists, but how well it has taken hold.

The value is in the structure. Because every domain is assessed against the same defined levels, the result is consistent between assessors, comparable across the organisation, and repeatable year on year. A score of "Level 3, Established" means the same thing this year as it will next year — which is precisely what an opinion can never guarantee.

Why a model beats an opinion

Ask five executives how well their agency is governed and you will get five answers, each shaped by where the person sits. A maturity model does not discard that judgement; it disciplines it. It forces the conversation onto defined criteria and, critically, onto the evidence behind each rating.

That is the feature that separates a credible model from a self-flattering one. Blue Zoo's approach scores every element twice: once for capability maturity — how well the practice is designed and embedded — and once for evidence confidence — how readily the organisation could prove it. The gap between the two is often the most useful finding of all, because it shows where governance is real and where it is merely believed.

Putting it to work

Six ways to make a maturity model earn its place

01Start with an honest baseline

A maturity model is only as useful as the candour behind it. Resist the urge to score where you would like to be. The first assessment exists to locate the starting line, not to reassure the agency — and an inflated baseline simply hides the very gaps the exercise is meant to reveal.

02Score capability and evidence separately

Rate how well each practice is designed and embedded, then rate how confidently you could prove it. Where the two diverge, you have found a governance practice that exists in belief but not in evidence.

03Aim for "established", not perfection

Level 5 is rarely the right target for every domain. For most agencies, reaching a consistent, well-evidenced Level 3 across the board is a more valuable and defensible goal than isolated pockets of excellence surrounded by weakness.

04Turn the scores into a roadmap

A maturity score is a diagnosis, not a destination. Its purpose is to generate a few prioritised actions that move a domain up a level, each sequenced by risk and given a named owner.

05Benchmark against peers

A score in isolation invites the question "compared to what?" Anonymised benchmarking against comparable agencies answers it — turning an internal number into context an agency can weigh.

06Re-measure to show movement

The real payoff comes the second time. Re-running the same assessment shows whether the roadmap is working, gives leadership an objective measure of progress, and demonstrates to the agency that governance is being actively managed.

The dual-scoring test: take any governance practice you are confident about and ask, "if the Auditor General asked us to prove this today, what would we hand over?" A strong capability score with a weak evidence score is not good governance yet — it is good intentions waiting to be documented.

Used well, a maturity model changes the governance conversation. It moves the agency from asking "do we think we are well governed?" to "here is where we are, here is where we are heading, and here is the evidence." That shift — from opinion to measurement — is what lets governance be managed like any other material part of the organisation.
