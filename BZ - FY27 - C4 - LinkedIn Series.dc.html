Post one · from "Reading the signals"
A five-minute test of governance capability

You don't need a new report to tell whether governance is lifting capability. You need to read the ones you already get, differently.

Take your three most recent board papers and read them as an outsider. Do they show options, trade-offs and the evidence behind the recommendation — or do they simply confirm a template was followed? A paper that reasons is a capability signal. A paper that merely complies is not.

One strong paper isn't proof. A consistent pattern across a run of them is.

Post two · from "Reading the signals"
A wall of green is not the same as a better outcome

It's easy to mistake activity for achievement. A dashboard full of green indicators tells you the machinery ran — not that anything improved for the public.

The signal to look for is different: a finding closed at its root cause rather than responded to and left to recur; a wait time that measurably fell rather than "all milestones met". Capability shows up in outcomes that changed, not tasks that were completed.

Next time a report lands, ask one question: what actually changed as a result?

Post three · from "Register to roadmap"
A maturity score is a diagnosis, not a destination

Plenty of agencies complete a governance maturity assessment and then file the result. The score was never the point — what you do next is.

The fastest way to turn ratings into action is to plot every improvement on impact against effort. Quick wins (high impact, low effort) build early momentum; major projects earn a place in the business plan; low-value, high-effort items get named and parked rather than left to drift.

Prioritise ruthlessly. A roadmap of six well-chosen actions beats a list of thirty no one owns.

Post four · from "Register to roadmap"
If it can't be tracked, it isn't a roadmap

Here's a simple test for any governance improvement plan: can a director read it in two minutes, know who owns each item, and see at the next review whether it moved?

Sequence the work across three horizons — Now, Next and Later — and give every action a named owner and a measure of "done". Keep the whole plan to a single page. That is the difference between a roadmap the Board can hold to account and a wish list that quietly stalls.

Set the re-measure date before you start. Movement is the proof.
