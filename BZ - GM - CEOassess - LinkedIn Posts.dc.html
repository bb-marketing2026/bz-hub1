Post 01 · The hook

The chief executive appraisal is the most consequential thing a board does each year. It's also, in most organisations, the least structured thing it does. Not through any failing — boards simply rarely have an instrument. So the discussion gets shaped by the most recent event, by the strength of individual relationships, and by whoever speaks first. Post 02 · Directors disagree

Here's something most boards discover the first time they rate the CEO against defined dimensions. Directors rate the same chief executive very differently. Not because anyone is wrong — because each director sees a different slice of the role. The one who sits on the audit committee sees governance discipline. The one who attends community events sees external presence. Neither sees the whole. Knowing that spread before the appraisal conversation is far better than discovering it during one. Post 04 · The remuneration link

A question every Chair should be able to answer without hesitation. On what documented basis was the chief executive's remuneration set this year? "The board discussed performance and formed a view" is honest, and it is also very thin — particularly in a public-sector or member-funded organisation where the decision may be examined by others. An appraisal rated against defined dimensions, with evidence recorded, converts a defensible instinct into a defensible decision. Post 05 · Recency bias

The single biggest distortion in CEO appraisal is recency. One difficult board meeting in November colours a judgement about twelve months of work. One well-received announcement does the same in the other direction. Every experienced Chair knows this and none of us is immune to it. Which is precisely why a structured framework matters — it forces attention across the whole role and the whole year, not the parts that are freshest. Free CEOassess assessment on one dimension. Post 06 · The CEO's view

An observation that surprises Chairs. Most chief executives welcome a structured appraisal — often more than the board does. Because very few have ever received one. They get impressions, encouragement and the occasional difficult conversation, but rarely a defined read on their own performance across the whole role, with anything actionable attached. Framed as development rather than judgement, CEOassess is as much an instrument for the CEO as for the board. That framing is also what makes it easy to introduce. Post 07 · CEO turnover

Unplanned CEO turnover is one of the most expensive things that can happen to an organisation. Strategy stalls. The executive team destabilises. Recruitment costs a year of momentum before the replacement has started. And it very rarely arrives without warning. What's usually missing is a mechanism that surfaces the warning early — clear expectations, honest structured feedback, and a conversation that happens annually rather than at crisis point. Good appraisal is retention infrastructure. Free CEOassess assessment on one dimension for Chairs.

Post 08 · Board engagement

One dimension of CEO performance that boards consistently under-rate: what actually reaches them. Are board papers sufficient to govern with? Do they present options and reasoning, or confirm that a process was followed? Does the board learn of problems from the CEO, or from somewhere else? That's not a reporting-template issue. It's a measurable dimension of chief-executive performance — and it's the one the board is best placed of anyone to assess honestly. Free CEOassess assessment on one dimension. Comment or DM.

Post 09 · New in the chair

If you've recently taken the chair, the first CEO appraisal is a defining moment — and an awkward one. You're assessing performance you only partly observed, against expectations someone else set, with a relationship you're still building. A defined framework helps considerably here. It gives you a structure that is visibly not personal, it makes the basis of your judgement explicit, and it establishes the instrument you'll use every year from now on. Post 10 · Capability vs evidence

A test worth applying to your own appraisal process. If your assessment of the chief executive were formally challenged — in a dispute, an inquiry, or simply by a new director asking how you reached it — what would you produce? A confident view with no record isn't a governance failure. But it isn't a defensible position either. CEOassess scores each dimension for maturity and for the confidence with which it could be evidenced. The gap between them is usually the most useful part. Post 11 · Trend, not verdict

The most valuable CEO appraisal isn't the first one. It's the third. A single assessment produces a verdict. Three assessments against the same dimensions produce a trajectory — which capabilities strengthened, which plateaued, and whether the development conversation from last year actually changed anything. Boards are comfortable reading financial trends and rarely have a performance trend for their most important appointment. Start the trend line.
